At 11:40 on a Tuesday night, my rack was pulling about a kilowatt. The UPS was fully charged, which sounds reassuring until you look one line over and see what that buys me under load. Ten minutes.

My power meter tells me what the rack draws, and my UPS tells me how long I could keep it running if the lights went out. That's one reason I like Hrafnholt, the read-only dashboard I built for my homelab.

On a monitor I can put the Plex map beside the transcode gauges and give a day's worth of history the whole row underneath. On my phone those same panels stack in the order I'll walk through here. <a href={desktop.src}>Open the full desktop screenshot</a> if you'd like to see how they fit together.

## Overview

The first four readings tell me what's moving through my internet connection, how much power the rack is drawing, how long the battery could carry it, and whether my services are responding. The two UniFi traces show upload and download separately. Farther down, Uptime Kuma counts seven services up and zero down. That's a quick enough check to do with the phone in one hand.

## Seven streams from Lincoln

The Plex map is my favorite part to look at. I love seeing those lines fan out from Lincoln on my desktop or my phone. On my phone, the map gets the full width of the screen, with the session details directly underneath. I can follow the teal line to Bismarck, then scroll down and see that a tvOS client there is playing a 1080p episode of _FBI_ directly from my server. Public Mode gives the viewer an alias. In this case, Dáin Ironfoot has an Apple TV.

Seven streams sounds busy, but those streams ask different things of the machine. For a direct play, Plex can send the file as it is. For a video transcode, the server has to convert the video as it plays. The split under the map gives me a reason to look at the GPU.

## Transcode engine

For a closer look at the Plex host, I have the RTX A2000 and the AMD EPYC 7282 side by side, even in portrait. The rings give me current GPU and CPU utilization. The smaller readings below tell me how busy the video encoder and decoder are, how much video memory is in use, and how hot the card is running.

## Compute fabric

My three Proxmox nodes are Gondor, Rohan, and Shire. The cluster has 282 GB of RAM between them, but that total hides how unevenly I've divided up the work. Gondor has 16 guests and is using 101 of its 126 GB. Rohan has the same amount of memory and only two guests. On the phone each node gets a row with its own CPU, memory, and storage bars, so it's easy to see where another guest might fit.

Emporia measures the server room and its air conditioning separately. In this snapshot the equipment was drawing 1,070 watts and the AC just 13. Over the day, though, cooling had already used 6.1 kWh. Putting those figures together helps explain the month-to-date total. By November 17 the lab and its cooling accounted for 51 percent of the electricity my house had used that month.

The UniFi power distribution unit breaks the rack total down by outlet. Erebor, my TrueNAS box, was drawing 323 watts in this screenshot. That's about a third of the 977 watts measured at the rack. Gondor, the Proxmox host just below it, was pulling another 212.

## Protection status

At the bottom I can check what happened overnight. The 3 a.m. Proxmox job backed up 18 guests successfully, and the Ironhills mirror finished its run for 15 datasets. Verification has its own count. Of the 566 restore points on my backup server, 410 had been checked and 156 were still pending. Seeing that count beside the successful jobs gives me a much better answer to “how are the backups?”

## What needs attention

The last panel is the operator watchlist. It calls out readings that cross their configured thresholds. In this capture the count is zero, even with some backup verification still pending. I like that I can see both facts on one phone screen and decide whether I need to open Proxmox Backup Server for a closer look.

I've been running a version of this dashboard in my homelab since July, and I'm pretty happy with it. I especially like how it looks on my phone. Even the Plex map feels at home in portrait.

If you'd like to see how it feels on your own phone, the public demo uses fictional data and opens straight in a browser.
